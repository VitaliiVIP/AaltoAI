# Explaining Rejection: Algorithmic Recourse and Counterfactual Explanations in AI Hiring

## Executive Summary

Technologies that tell rejected job candidates *what they would need to change to pass* — "algorithmic recourse" or actionable counterfactual explanations — exist in research and in narrow commercial forms, but as of September 2026 no mainstream hiring platform delivers true, validated, threshold-crossing recourse ("do X and you would have been hired"). What exists commercially is softer: developmental "insights" and coaching feedback tied to assessments (HireVue's Candidate Insights Report, Sapia.ai's "My Insights"/SAIGE, Arctic Shores feedback reports) that describe traits and offer generic improvement tips but explicitly disclaim being the reason for a decision. The academic machinery to build genuine recourse is mature (counterfactual explanations, actionable recourse, causal recourse, robustness and anti-gaming methods), and there is one documented real-world hiring deployment (CounteRGAN on the Hired marketplace). But a convergence of legal, technical, economic, and validity problems means honest recourse against a real hiring bar is very hard, and in most hiring settings the "bar" is relative (rank- and headcount-based), which makes threshold recourse partly fictional.

Regulation is the main force pushing feedback/explanation into hiring: the EU AI Act (employment AI is high-risk under Annex III; obligations now deferred to 2 December 2027 by the Digital Omnibus), GDPR Article 22, NYC Local Law 144 (bias audits + notices), the revised Colorado AI Act (SB 26-189, effective 1 January 2027, adds an adverse-decision explanation right), Illinois' AI Video Interview Act and HB 3773, and litigation such as Mobley v. Workday. Our judgment: a **defensible, well-scoped implementation is feasible for structured, skills-based assessments** where features are candidate-actionable and the decision is threshold-based, but it should be framed as developmental coaching, not as a legal statement of "the reason," and should never be deployed on relative/ranked decisions or noisy black-box screeners without major caveats.

## Key Findings

- **Commercial reality:** Vendor "feedback" is overwhelmingly developmental self-insight, not recourse. HireVue's Candidate Insights Report, Sapia.ai's My Insights/SAIGE coaching, and Arctic Shores' feedback reports give trait descriptions and "development tips," are often candidate-only (not shared with the employer), and explicitly state receipt "does not indicate a decision has been made." None publish evidence that following their tips changes a future hiring outcome. Recruiter-facing explainability (fit scores, competency scores, "explainable scores") is more common than candidate-facing recourse.
- **One documented hiring recourse deployment exists:** Nemirovsky et al. applied a GAN-based counterfactual generator (CounteRGAN) to the *Hired* marketplace's ML model to give candidates real-time "actionable feedback" (WSDM 2021). This is the exception that proves the rule — it is a marketplace-success predictor, not an employer hiring gate.
- **Research is ready but warns against naïve use:** Wachter et al. (2017), Ustun et al. (2019), Mothilal et al. (2020, DiCE), Karimi et al. (recourse survey; causal recourse), Poyiadzi et al. (FACE), Pawelczyk et al. (CARLA) supply the algorithms. But Barocas, Selbst & Raghavan (2020) show counterfactual explanations rest on hidden, often false assumptions; Upadhyay et al. (ROAR) and Dominguez-Olmedo et al. show recourse is fragile to model updates and adversarial shifts; Hardt et al. (strategic classification) and performative-prediction work show recourse invites gaming that degrades the screener.
- **Regulation motivates but does not yet mandate counterfactual recourse.** Transparency/notice and (in Colorado and under GDPR) a right to *an explanation of the decision* and human review are emerging, but none require telling candidates *how to become hireable*. The EU AI Act's high-risk employment obligations were deferred to 2 December 2027 by the Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744).
- **The strongest objection is structural, not technical:** most hiring decisions are relative (rank against other applicants, fixed headcount, hiring-manager judgment), so there is no fixed threshold to give recourse against; the advice would be conditional on a candidate pool the applicant cannot see and that changes every cycle. Where screeners are also weak predictors of job performance, recourse optimizes a noisy proxy.

## Part 1 — Review of Existing Solutions and the Regulatory Landscape

### 1.1 What "recourse" would mean here vs. what vendors actually do

Algorithmic recourse (Ustun, Spangher & Liu 2019) is the ability of a person to change a model's decision by altering *actionable* inputs; a counterfactual explanation (Wachter, Mittelstadt & Russell 2017) states the smallest change to the inputs that flips the outcome. Applied to hiring, true recourse would say: *"Had you had X (a certification, a specific skill, N months more of Y), the screening model would have advanced you; here is how to acquire X."* Almost no commercial product does this against an actual hiring decision. Instead vendors provide (a) recruiter-facing explainability (why a candidate scored as they did) and/or (b) candidate-facing *developmental* feedback (trait insights and generic tips), carefully decoupled from the hire/no-hire decision.

**Bottom line on vendors:** the market has converged on *developmental self-insight* and *recruiter-side explainability*, deliberately avoiding statements that connect feedback to the rejection (to limit legal exposure). The only documented true-recourse hiring deployment is research-grade (below).

### 1.3 The one documented real-world hiring recourse deployment

- **Nemirovsky, D., Thiebaut, N., Xu, Y., & Gupta, A. (2021). "Providing Actionable Feedback in Hiring Marketplaces using Generative Adversarial Networks." WSDM '21, pp. 1089–1092.** Applied a GAN-based counterfactual generator to *Hired*, "one of the world's largest hiring platforms," to give candidates real-time actionable feedback to improve their marketplace success, with reported >1000× latency gains vs. baselines. Underlying method: **CounteRGAN** (Nemirovsky et al., arXiv:2009.05199, 2020; UAI 2022, PMLR v180:1488–1497), a residual-GAN producing realistic, actionable counterfactuals in real time.
- Significance: proves feasibility of real-time recourse in a hiring-adjacent ML system — but the predicted target was marketplace success, not an employer's hire/no-hire gate, so it sidesteps the "relative bar" problem central to actual hiring.

### 1.4 Regulatory landscape

**EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689).** Employment/recruitment AI (screening, filtering, evaluating candidates) is **high-risk under Annex III**. High-risk obligations include risk management, data governance, technical documentation, human oversight, transparency to deployers, accuracy/robustness, and EU-database registration; deployers in some cases must run a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment. Crucially, **Article 86 gives affected persons a right to an explanation of individual decisions** made with high-risk systems. Timeline: the Act entered into force 1 August 2024; Article 50 transparency duties and GPAI enforcement took effect 2 August 2026; the **Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) deferred standalone Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027** (Annex I product-embedded to 2 August 2028). Fines up to €15M or 3% of turnover for high-risk breaches. The right to explanation is about the *decision*, not about *how to become hireable* — recourse would go beyond the legal floor.

**GDPR Article 22 + Articles 13–15.** Right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal/significant effects, with rights to obtain human intervention, express a view, and contest; "meaningful information about the logic involved." Wachter, Mittelstadt & Russell (2017) argue counterfactual explanations are a good fit for these aims (understand, contest, and know what to change) without opening the black box.

**NYC Local Law 144 (AEDT law).** Since enforcement on 5 July 2023: employers using automated employment decision tools for NYC roles must (1) commission an independent annual bias audit, (2) publicly post an audit summary, and (3) give candidates ≥10 business days' notice plus the job qualifications/characteristics the tool assesses. On request, employers must disclose data type, source, and retention. Penalties $500–$1,500 per violation per day. Compliance has been very low in practice: Wright, Muenster et al., "Null Compliance: NYC Local Law 144 and the Challenges of Algorithm Accountability" (ACM FAccT 2024), reported that of **391 employers recorded by 155 student investigators, only 18 posted audit reports and 13 posted transparency notices.** LL 144 requires *notice and audit*, not recourse.

**Colorado AI Act.** The original 2024 CAIA (SB 24-205) was repealed and replaced by **SB 26-189 (signed 14 May 2026, effective 1 January 2027)**. It regulates "covered automated decision-making technology (ADMT)" in "consequential decisions" (expressly including employment and job applicants). Deployers must give pre-use notice and, **after an adverse decision, provide within 30 days a plain-language description/explanation of the decision, the right to correct data, and the right to appeal for meaningful human review.** This adverse-decision explanation is the closest US statutory analog to recourse, but still explanation-of-decision, not how-to-qualify.

**Illinois.** The **AI Video Interview Act (820 ILCS 42, P.A. 101-260, effective 1 January 2020)** requires, before AI-analyzed video interviews: (1) notice that AI may be used, (2) an explanation of "how the artificial intelligence works and what general types of characteristics it uses to evaluate applicants," and (3) consent (an employer "may not use artificial intelligence to evaluate applicants who have not consented"); it also limits video sharing and requires deletion within 30 days of an applicant's request. **P.A. 102-47 (effective 1 January 2022)** added Section 20: demographic (race/ethnicity) reporting to the Department of Commerce and Economic Opportunity for employers who rely *solely* on AI to decide who advances to in-person interviews, with DCEO analysis reported to the Governor and General Assembly by July 1 each year on whether the data discloses racial bias. **HB 3773 / P.A. 103-0804 amends the Illinois Human Rights Act (effective 1 January 2026)** to bar AI use that has the effect of discriminating against protected classes or that uses zip code as a proxy, with an employee-notice duty.

**EEOC.** The EEOC's **May 12, 2022 ADA technical assistance** ("The Americans with Disabilities Act and the Use of Software, Algorithms, and Artificial Intelligence to Assess Job Applicants and Employees") warned that AI tools can unlawfully "screen out" people with disabilities and must allow accommodation. The **May 18, 2023 Title VII technical assistance** ("Select Issues: Assessing Adverse Impact in Software, Algorithms, and Artificial Intelligence Used in Employment Selection Procedures Under Title VII") applied the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures and the four-fifths (80%) rule to algorithmic selection, stressing that the four-fifths rule is "merely a rule of thumb" (not a safe harbor) and that employers remain liable for vendor-built tools. **EEOC v. iTutorGroup, Inc. (No. 1:22-cv-02565, E.D.N.Y.)** was the EEOC's first AI/automated-screening hiring settlement: per the EEOC newsroom (settlement 9 Aug 2023; consent decree approved 8 Sept 2023), iTutorGroup agreed to **pay $365,000 to approximately 200 rejected job seekers age 40 and over** after software auto-rejected female applicants 55+ and male applicants 60+ — though this was disparate-treatment via hard-coded age cutoffs, not ML disparate impact. Under the new administration, the **EEOC removed its AI technical-assistance documents from its website in late January 2025** (reported ~27 Jan 2025); the underlying statutes (Title VII, ADA, ADEA) and the UGESP/four-fifths framework are unchanged.

**Mobley v. Workday (N.D. Cal., No. 3:23-cv-00770).** Derek Mobley alleges Workday's AI screening tools discriminated by race, age, and disability across 100+ applications. Key rulings: the court held Workday can be liable as an "agent" of employers; **May 16, 2025** conditional ADEA collective certification; **February 17, 2026** authorization of collective notice (applicants 40+ who applied via Workday since 24 Sept 2020); per Forbes (29 May 2026), **approximately 14,000 people opted in by the 7 March 2026 deadline** ("a figure Workday declined to confirm"), and court records note Workday's platform processed **roughly 1.1 billion job applications during the covered period**; **May/June 2026** discovery rulings (Workday's bias-testing data shielded by attorney-client privilege; customers' applicant data not in Workday's "control"); **June 22, 2026** core discrimination claims allowed to proceed. Implications: it establishes vendor exposure and — relevant to recourse — signals that *explanations become discoverable evidence*.

**Other relevant EU instruments.** The Platform Work Directive (adopted 2024) creates algorithmic-management transparency and human-review rights for platform workers; the Pay Transparency Directive (2023, transposition by 2026) may indirectly pressure feedback/justification practices. Neither mandates hiring recourse.

### 2.2 Concrete implementation architecture

A defensible pipeline, staged:

1. **Screening/scoring model (upstream).** Prefer an interpretable, threshold-based, skills-based scorer (or a monotonic/linear model) over an opaque ensemble/LLM. Recourse is only honest if the decision is a genuine threshold on candidate-actionable features. Log the exact model version used for each decision.
2. **Recourse generator.** For linear/scored models use Ustun et al.'s integer-programming flipsets; for black-box use DiCE/CERTIFAI/CARLA methods. Enforce: **actionability** (only mutable features — skills, certifications, portfolio, measured competencies; never age, protected traits, or proxies), **immutability/protected-feature exclusion**, **causal constraints** (Karimi; von Kügelgen) so advice changes the true qualification not a proxy, **plausibility/feasibility** (FACE; data-manifold), **diversity** (DiCE — multiple paths), and **cost** (least-effort, group-equalized per Gupta et al.).
3. **Skill-gap → learning-resource mapping.** Map the counterfactual "missing" features to a skills taxonomy (ESCO/O*NET/Lightcast, LinkedIn Skills Graph) and then to concrete courses/credentials/experiences. This converts "you needed X" into "here is how to get X."
4. **Candidate-facing explanation (UX).** Present as ranked reason codes + one or two feasible counterfactual paths + resources, in plain language (Bove et al.), framed as developmental coaching. Given IUI-2025 evidence, prefer ranked reason codes with resource links over a single counterfactual, and disclose uncertainty and that the decision was also relative to other candidates.
5. **Validation / robustness / gaming defenses.** Validate recourse *validity* under retraining (ROAR; robust recourse); test adversarial robustness (Dominguez-Olmedo); separate genuine improvement from gaming using causal features (Fokkema; Hardt strategic classification); monitor performative drift (Perdomo); rate-limit disclosure granularity to avoid keyword-stuffing arms races; and audit recourse-cost fairness across groups (Gupta; von Kügelgen; AReS global rules). Keep a human-in-the-loop review path (GDPR Art. 22; Colorado; EU AI Act human oversight).

## Part 3 — Why This Might Not Work (Critical Analysis)

**(a) Legal risk.** Explanations are *discoverable evidence*. If a counterfactual repeatedly surfaces features correlated with protected classes (e.g., gaps in employment, "communication style," a proxy like zip code), it can document disparate impact — the exact exposure highlighted by Mobley v. Workday and EEOC's 2023 Title VII guidance. Barocas, Selbst & Raghavan (2020) show feature-highlighting explanations embed subjective choices about what is mutable and what the baseline is, so any stated "reason" can be contested as arbitrary or pretextual. Additional risks: **defamation/privacy** (telling a candidate something false or sensitive about themselves), **trade-secret** tension (explanations vs. protecting the model — though Wachter et al. argue counterfactuals avoid model disclosure), **negligence** (advice a candidate relies on that is wrong), and the requirement that explanations be **true** — hard for noisy, ensemble, or LLM-based screeners whose "reasons" are post-hoc rationalizations. *Mitigation:* structured, documented, job-related criteria produce a *more* defensible reason than silence (HeyMilo/Criteria argument), and counterfactuals can be constrained to job-related, protected-feature-free variables — but this only works for well-specified models.

**(b) Technical.** Recourse **validity decays under model retraining and distribution shift** (ROAR; Dominguez-Olmedo): advice given today may not flip tomorrow's model. **Non-uniqueness / the Rashomon effect**: many equally good models give different recourse, so "the reason" is not well-defined. Recourse is **sensitive to feature representation** and to correl/causal confusion — changing a *proxy* feature (Barocas et al.; Karimi causal recourse) doesn't change the true outcome. For **unstructured resume text and LLM screeners**, there is no clean feature space over which to compute a counterfactual, so recourse degenerates into "add keywords." **Immutable/protected features** may be doing real predictive work, making feasible recourse impossible for some candidates (Ustun et al. show classifiers can deny universal recourse). **Cost asymmetries** mean recourse can be far more expensive for disadvantaged groups (recourse-fairness problem).

**(c) Gaming / strategic behavior.** Publishing what to change invites Goodhart's-law gaming (Hardt et al., strategic classification; Tsirtsis & Gomez-Rodriguez): candidates optimize resumes to the model (keyword stuffing), which **degrades the screener's validity** and triggers an arms race and performative drift (Perdomo et al.). Fokkema et al. show recourse and manipulation can be formally indistinguishable unless advice targets *causal* qualifications. This is the core tension: useful recourse and gameable recourse are often the same disclosure.

**(d) Organizational / economic — the strongest objection.** Employers have **little incentive** to help rejected candidates and face cost, liability, and volume constraints. Glassdoor data shows an average corporate opening attracts about **250 applications, of which only 4–6 candidates are interviewed** — so most applicants are rejected by design. (Note: the frequently cited claim that "75% of resumes are auto-rejected by ATS before a human sees them" appears unsubstantiated — Enhancv/ApplyMate traced it to defunct 2012 Preptel marketing, and a late-2025 Enhancv survey of 25 recruiters found 92% said their systems do not auto-reject resumes; treat it as folklore.) More fundamentally, **the "bar" is usually relative, not absolute**: hiring is rank-based against other applicants, capped by headcount, and shaped by hiring-manager taste. Recourse to a fixed threshold is therefore partly **fictional** — the honest counterfactual is "you would have advanced *in that specific pool*," which changes every cycle and depends on candidates the applicant cannot see. This makes stable, truthful recourse structurally impossible for most competitive requisitions. (For scale, Workday's platform reportedly processed roughly 1.1 billion applications during the Mobley covered period — the volumes at which any recourse feature must operate.)

**(e) Validity of the underlying screener.** Raghavan et al. (2020) document that many screening tools have weak or undisclosed validation against actual job performance. If the screener is a poor predictor, recourse **advises optimizing a noisy proxy** — potentially harming candidates by sending them to acquire skills that don't actually matter, while lending the tool false legitimacy.

**(f) Human / psychological.** AI feedback is perceived as less fair on job-relatedness and two-way communication (Acikgoz et al. 2020; Gilliland's justice model), and telling people to change **near-immutable characteristics** (personality traits, "communication style," accent) can be harmful and demoralizing. Over-trust is a risk: candidates may treat probabilistic, noisy advice as authoritative. The IUI-2025 hiring study found counterfactual advice can produce *worse* outcomes than simpler reason codes when the system is ignorant of the candidate's real constraints. Yet Sapia.ai reports high candidate satisfaction with developmental feedback (an average 9.05/10 satisfaction rating, with 8 in 10 candidates leaving voluntary written feedback, per its 2025 Humanising Hiring Report) — suggesting *framing as coaching* (not as the rejection reason) is what candidates value.

**(g) Empirical evidence of low uptake / failure.** Vendors keep candidate feedback optional and decouple it from decisions; NYC LL 144 compliance is near-nil (18 of 391 employers posted audit reports in the Null Compliance study); EEOC pulled its AI guidance in 2025; and no vendor publishes evidence that following its feedback changes a subsequent hiring outcome. The only true hiring-recourse deployment (Hired/CounteRGAN) predicted marketplace success, not employer hiring.

**Counterarguments / mitigations (brief).** Recourse *can* work in **narrow, well-specified settings**: skills-based, threshold-gated assessments (e.g., "pass the coding screen"), certifications, or clearly job-related competencies, where features are candidate-actionable and causally tied to performance. Framing feedback as **developmental coaching plus learning resources** (not as "the legal reason") captures most procedural-justice benefits while limiting exposure. Causal, protected-feature-free, group-cost-equalized, robustness-tested recourse addresses many technical/fairness objections. And regulation (Colorado adverse-decision explanations, EU Art. 86, GDPR Art. 22) will likely push employers toward *some* explanation, making a well-designed recourse layer a compliance and employer-brand asset rather than a pure cost.

## Recommendations

1. **Scope tightly. Deploy recourse only where the decision is a genuine, candidate-actionable threshold** (skills assessments, coding screens, certification gates). **Do not deploy** threshold-style recourse on rank/headcount-limited requisitions, opaque ensembles, or LLM resume screeners — there the honest answer is "the decision was relative," and false recourse creates legal and ethical risk. *Threshold to change:* if the decision is provably relative, switch from recourse to transparency + human-review offer only.
2. **Frame as developmental coaching, not "the reason for rejection."** Follow HireVue/Sapia/Arctic Shores practice: candidate-owned insights, plain language, explicit statement that it is guidance, not a determinative reason. This preserves procedural-justice benefits (Gilliland; Acikgoz) while limiting defamation/discovery exposure.
3. **Engineer for causality, actionability, and fairness.** Use Ustun-style flipsets or DiCE with hard constraints: exclude protected features and proxies (zip code — explicitly barred by Illinois HB 3773), enforce causal constraints (Karimi; von Kügelgen), equalize recourse cost across groups (Gupta), and give diverse feasible paths (DiCE/FACE).
4. **Prefer ranked reason codes + learning resources over single counterfactuals.** Per IUI-2025 evidence, map gaps to ESCO/O*NET/Lightcast skills and concrete courses/credentials; disclose uncertainty.
5. **Build robustness and anti-gaming in from day one.** Test recourse validity across retraining (ROAR), test adversarial robustness (Dominguez-Olmedo), throttle disclosure granularity, monitor performative drift (Perdomo), and separate improvement from gaming via causal features (Fokkema). *Threshold to change:* if screener AUC/validity drops after recourse launch, reduce disclosure granularity or suspend.
6. **Validate the screener against job performance before giving any recourse.** If the model isn't a demonstrated predictor of performance (Raghavan et al.), do not advise candidates to optimize it. *Threshold:* require documented criterion validity before candidate-facing advice.
7. **Align to the strictest applicable regime and keep humans in the loop.** Prepare for EU AI Act high-risk obligations by 2 December 2027 (Art. 86 explanation, human oversight, FRIA), GDPR Art. 22 human-review, Colorado's 30-day adverse-decision explanation (1 Jan 2027), Illinois notice/consent and HB 3773, and NYC LL 144 audits/notices. Treat every explanation as discoverable — document job-relatedness and version control.
8. **Pilot and measure.** Run a controlled pilot measuring candidate fairness perceptions, reapplication success, adverse-impact ratios, and screener validity drift before scaling. *Threshold to expand:* improved justice perceptions **and** no validity degradation **and** no widening of group recourse-cost gaps.

## Caveats

- **Recency/enforcement flux:** AI-hiring law is moving fast and some items are contested or deferred — the EU AI Act high-risk timeline was pushed to 2 December 2027 by the 2026 Digital Omnibus; Colorado's law was rewritten before taking effect; EEOC guidance was withdrawn in 2025 though the statutes stand. Treat all dates as of September 2026.
- **Vendor claims are largely unverified marketing.** Feedback-quality, satisfaction, and "bias-free" claims (Sapia.ai, HireVue, Arctic Shores) come from vendor materials and press releases; independent validation is scarce, and Raghavan et al. document sector-wide opacity. HireVue/Sapia explicitly limit feedback to candidate-owned developmental insight, not decision reasons.
- **"Recourse" vs. "feedback" conflation:** Much of what is marketed as candidate feedback is trait self-insight, not validated recourse; we have flagged where products stop short of true recourse.
- **The Hired/CounteRGAN deployment** targeted marketplace-success prediction, not an employer's hire/no-hire gate; its success does not establish that recourse works against real hiring bars.
- **Widely cited statistics debunked:** the "75% of resumes auto-rejected by ATS" figure is unsubstantiated (see Part 3d); use per-opening application volumes (Glassdoor's ~250 applications, 4–6 interviewed) instead.
- **Some cited works are venue/date-approximate:** a few citation details (exact pages/venues for a handful of secondary papers such as Kelly-Lyth, Bove et al., Fokkema et al., Langer & Landers, Mirowska, and the Poyiadzi/Verma surveys) are drawn from secondary references and should be verified against the primary source before publication.
